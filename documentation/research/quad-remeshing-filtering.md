in quad remeshing, what kinds of prefiltering are useful? prefiltering the mesh, prefiltering the curvature field, etc?

## Claude

The most useful framing here is that "prefiltering the mesh" and "prefiltering the field" are often competing rather than complementary, and you usually want the second more than the first. The reason is the derivative-order hierarchy: positions are 0th order, normals 1st, and curvature/principal directions 2nd. Each derivative multiplies high-frequency noise by frequency, so the cross field — being built from second-order data — is the noisiest thing in the whole pipeline even when the surface looks clean. That argues for spending your filtering budget on the field, and being conservative about touching geometry, because geometric denoising that's aggressive enough to clean up curvature will also round off the features you wanted the quads to align to.
So, by stage:
Mesh-level prefiltering. The high-value operations are the ones that improve conditioning without destroying features: isotropic pre-remeshing (Botsch–Kobbelt incremental) so the cotangent Laplacian and any FEM operators aren't sitting on slivers, topological cleanup (cap holes, strip handles/topological noise, kill non-manifold edges and degenerate triangles), and decimation of pathologically dense scan regions. The low-value or actively harmful operation is strong geometric denoising (bilateral, L0, normal-filtering flows) applied with the goal of smoothing curvature — it couples feature loss to noise reduction. If you must denoise geometry, prefer feature-preserving normal filtering and keep it mild; then do the real smoothing on the derived field where it's reversible and feature-aware.
Curvature / direction-field prefiltering. This is where the leverage is, and there are a few things worth being deliberate about:

Filter tensors, not directions. Principal directions carry the π-ambiguity (and a 4-RoSy cross field the π/2 ambiguity), so you can't naively average them. Build a per-element symmetric tensor — the shape operator, or a structure tensor formed from outer products of estimated principal directions weighted by curvature magnitude — and diffuse those. Tensors are sign-invariant, positive (semi)definite, and average cleanly; you re-extract eigenvectors afterward. Equivalently, lift to the power-k representation (z⁴ for cross fields, à la Knöppel/Diamanti's representation vectors) which linearizes the symmetry and lets you smooth with an ordinary complex Laplacian.
Scale is the real filter. The integration radius over which you estimate curvature is a low-pass cutoff. Integral-invariant / normal-cycle (Cohen-Steiner–Morvan) curvature with a ball radius r gives you an explicit, tunable scale-space, which is far more principled than fitting a quadric to a fixed one-ring and then smoothing the result. Choose r to match the feature scale you want the quads to resolve; that single choice does most of the prefiltering.
Confidence-weight rather than mask. In umbilic/flat regions (|κ₁−κ₂| → 0) the principal directions are undefined and will be pure noise. Don't hard-threshold them out — weight the curvature-alignment term by anisotropy, e.g. (κ₁−κ₂)², so the smoothness energy takes over smoothly where curvature is uninformative. This is the soft analogue of prefiltering and avoids seams at the mask boundary.
Anisotropic diffusion for the field. If you want to smooth along a feature line without bleeding across it, a Perona-Malik-style anisotropic diffusion on the field (or a feature-aware weighting in the smoothness energy) preserves the directional discontinuity you actually care about.

The smoothness-vs-alignment weight in the field energy is itself the master regularization knob: crank smoothness and you get the globally-smoothest field with the fewest singularities; crank alignment and you track curvature faithfully but inherit its noise and spurious singularities.
Sizing / metric-field prefiltering. If you're doing adaptive density, the sizing field derived from curvature must be gradient-limited (Lipschitz / gradation control, e.g. Alauzet's bounded gradation). Unfiltered, element size jumps between neighbors and you get skewed quads at the transitions; clamping |∇h| ≤ some bound spreads the transition over several elements. For anisotropic quads the same applies to the full metric tensor — smooth/regularize the Riemannian metric and intersect it with the gradation constraint.
Feature-graph prefiltering. Sharp features become hard alignment constraints, so the feature detection wants its own cleanup: dihedral-angle thresholding with hysteresis (high/low thresholds) to avoid flicker, then chaining edges into polylines, snapping near-junctions, and pruning short spurs before they're fed to the field as constraints. A noisy feature graph injects garbage constraints that no amount of field smoothing will fix.
One downstream thing worth noting because it's really "filtering" in disguise: spurious singularity pairs from residual field noise can be cancelled in a post-pass (close index +¼/−¼ pairs), and global methods like QuadriFlow's min-cost-flow over the Instant-Meshes field do exactly this kind of singularity regularization. If you've filtered the field well upstream, there's much less of this to clean up.
